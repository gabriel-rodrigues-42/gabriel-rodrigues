What I believe

The radio out of tune

The Earth is 4.54 billion years old, according to the US Geological Survey (USGS). Humans as we are today have been around for about 300,000 years.

If the whole history of the Earth fit into one year, each second would be worth about 144 years. The religions we know today would appear in the last half minute of December 31.

This takes nothing away from anyone's faith. It only shows that the planet is much older than our arguments about it.

Teenage years · Inhumas

Where my view comes from

I was baptized in an evangelical church as a teenager. I was lost, and there I found a path: to seek the good and help others. I was never religious by tradition. My parents went to church only a few times.

Around then I broke my arm skateboarding after drinking. I got a titanium plate, which is still in my arm. I did physical therapy, but my hand never got back 100% of its movement. That was when I saw that I owned my actions and what could happen if I kept going that way.

What I never accepted is that the world is just chance. I believe that someone able to create worlds wants their creation to understand what is around it.

An image

The radio

I think of God as a radio station that never went off the air. We talk to Him. We pray, we ask, we give thanks. But our radio still can't pick up the station properly.

Maybe science will help us turn the dial. Maybe the answer already reaches each person's heart. Either way, it is up to us to decide what to do with our lives.

No study proves this, and I don't say it does. It is what I believe.

The hard question

What about people who suffer through no fault of their own?

I don't believe God chooses who will die. A lot of pain comes from human actions with consequences no one foresaw.

In September 1987, in Goiânia, a device containing caesium-137 was broken open and the part was sold to a scrapyard. Four people died. The waste is stored in Abadia de Goiás, 23 km from the capital.

Memory

We forget

The memory of a disaster depends on the people who lived through it. A study of a thousand years of floods in the Czech Republic showed that it fades within two generations. After that, villages start building near the rivers again (Nature Communications, 2019).

In Porto Alegre, the Guaíba reached 4.75 metres in 1941. In May 2024, it went past 5 metres. Eighty-three years passed between the two floods. Almost no one who lived through the first was still here to warn us.

Today we record almost everything. What fails is turning the record into a rule.

In Japan, an old stone in the village of Aneyoshi says:

“Remember the calamity of the great tsunamis. Do not build any homes below this point.”

I want something similar for Brazil, which I call Memory Markers:

  • a plaque at the highest level of each disaster, on the street, where everyone can see it;
  • an open online map with every plaque, its date and its source;
  • a rule: any public work or new housing development below the mark has to explain in writing why it is safe.

Brazil's Civil Defence Law (Law 12,608/2012) already requires each city to map risk areas and forbid new settlement in them. It says nothing about marking how far past disasters reached. This way, memory no longer depends on the people who lived through the disaster.

Together

With religion or without

In Brazil, 56.7% of people are Catholic, 26.9% are evangelical and 9.3% have no religion. In the Centre-West region, evangelicals reach 31.4% (IBGE, 2022 Census).

Humanity has come together before. On 8 May 1980, the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated, after vaccination around the whole world. No one asked the religion of the people who got the vaccine.

We can talk to God and believe. But we have to take charge of our lives and fight for a better world. Our job is to seek knowledge together.
