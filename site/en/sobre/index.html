About

From Inhumas to the world. And back to Goiás.

I'm Gabriel Rodrigues. I was born in Inhumas, Goiás, on January 25, 1993. I've been a software developer for more than ten years.

This is the story of how a boy who did the change in his head at a snack stall decided to go into politics.

Age 12 · Rural Goiás

Change in my head

I come from a humble family. We sold coxinha and pastel, Brazilian fried snacks, and lived month to month.

I started working at 12. We went from town to town, from festival to festival. I was good at making change and I remembered what each person had ordered.

My mother still makes snacks and sells them at the Sunday market in Inhumas. My brother has a stall there too.

Teenage years · Inhumas

500 players and a rough night

Before I had a computer, I went to the LAN house. I bought my first computer with money I saved collecting cans and working as a young apprentice, bagging groceries at a supermarket.

Still a teenager, I set up a server for Lineage 2, an online game. At its peak it had more than 500 people playing at the same time.

I worked on the code, built the website, hunted cheaters. One event night, more people showed up than the machine could handle. I fixed it with everyone waiting.

Nobody had told me that was engineering. It felt like taking care of a small town.

2014 to 2018 · Goiânia

Who is on the other side of the screen

In 2014 I started a degree in Systems Analysis and Development at PUC Goiás. I paid for college with FIES, the federal student loan program. That same year I started working at a software company in Goiânia.

I built the web version of a debt collection system. There, people in debt could negotiate their own debt without a phone call full of pressure.

One day, while testing the system, I found a familiar name on the list of debtors. It was someone from my family.

It's hard to know your family needs money and you can't help. I'll tell that story in full one day, calmly.

Since then I don't just ask "does it work?". I ask "who is on the other side?".

Back then I also answered programming questions on Stack Overflow in Portuguese. I became one of the top 50 contributors of all time. Teaching became half of my job.

2017 · Malta

The water really is salty

I earned R$ 2,500 a month as a junior developer. My FIES debt was over R$ 60,000. By my math, I would only finish paying it off in 2032.

So I thought: I'm young, I want to see the world and learn English. I went to work abroad with a plan and a date to come back:

  • learn a new culture and English;
  • save money and pay off FIES;
  • help my family and travel.

I grew up watching Glória Maria, a Brazilian TV journalist, showing the world as it is. I saw the sea for the first time in Malta, in 2017, at 24. My first reaction was to taste it: the water really is salty.

Snow I only saw at 31, in Sierra Nevada, Spain.

Working in a second language was hard. I thought people were judging my English, not my work. The doubt went away when a team lead gave me a real project to own. Trust fixed what no speech would have fixed.

2018 to 2026 · Malta, Spain and remote work

Building to protect

I went to Europe thinking I'd work on video games. I ended up in iGaming, the online betting market.

I had two choices. Go back and look for a job in a Goiânia that, in 2017, had few openings for what I did. Or understand the market and build better tools inside it. When your family needs you, every work decision weighs.

I stayed. And I chose the part that protects people: identity verification, deposit and loss limits, self-exclusion, money laundering alerts.

The turning point

Compliance without law protects no one

It wasn't a single day. It was many small things.

The first company I worked for in Europe was bought by a bigger one and slowly taken apart. All the care for player protection was swallowed with it.

Then I saw Brazil legalize betting without serious tools to prevent abuse. In Europe, I saw countries tightening the rules. Here, I saw influencers selling bets as if they were investments.

The money of those who need it most was draining away. And the hard work of compliance teams is worth nothing if, at the top, marketing sees that it's profitable and that nobody gets punished.

I saw the internet I grew up with become a place built for profit, not for people's well-being. I realized that good code doesn't beat a bad rule. Rules are changed in politics.

2020 to 2021 · From afar

The pandemic

In the middle of the pandemic, my parents were in a car accident. I was on the other side of the ocean. I sold all my shares to help.

At the same time, I watched the country suffer from decisions made without science. Medicine with no proven efficacy became a recommendation. Misinformation ran wild.

The federal government left Pfizer's first three vaccine offers unanswered, in August 2020. In January 2021, hospitals in Manaus ran out of oxygen and patients died because of it.

I don't write this to point fingers at anyone. I write it because it's the biggest example I've seen of a system that decided without data and without listening to those who know. That costs lives. We can do better.

2026 · Goiânia

Why politics

On a podcast, I heard a sentence from someone who had been in power that never left my head:

"The honest politician you want is inside you."

It hit me because criticizing is easy. What's hard is being in power and deciding for an entire society.

And I know my weakness: software engineers usually aren't great communicators. It's a barrier I want to face, in public.

One inspiration has been with me for years. In the book The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, a supercomputer answers the ultimate question of life, the universe and everything with a number: 42.

In computer code, 42 is the asterisk (*), the symbol that stands for anything. To me, that's the answer: life is what you make of it.

What engineering taught me

  1. Find the root cause. You don't fix a problem by repeating the solution that already doesn't work. First investigate, understand, measure.
  2. Speak clearly with those affected. In software, they're the users. In government, it's the people.
  3. Change a little, test and repeat. Small, incremental, measured steps that add up to a big impact.

A lot in government works like a black box: money goes in and nobody can properly audit it, or nobody trusts the audit, because whoever audits has an interest in not seeing. We can learn from countries that open their data and audit better.

Digital security

I want to be society's blue team

In information security there are two teams. One attacks to find the flaws. The other defends the system and fixes it.

Red team

Those who attack democracy. Those who want power concentrated in a few hands. Those who treat Brazil only as a market to extract income from and leave.

Blue team

Those who close the loopholes, open the data, measure the results and protect the system so it serves everyone.

I want to be on the blue team, for the public good.

The hard question

Why trust me?

I spent eight years abroad. I hold no office and belong to no party. Trust has to be earned, and I know that.

I came back to Goiás because I can't watch the place where I was born suffer from problems I know can be solved.

I don't want a term of pointing fingers. I want to look at the systems, understand each situation and improve it, through dialogue and with the help of those who live the problem.

My goal is to run for a seat as federal representative for Goiás in 2030. Until then, I study in public: every idea is on this site, with sources and open to criticism.
