Proposal under study

The money to reindustrialize Brazil already exists. It's just sitting still.

People with large fortunes already borrow from banks using their own shares as collateral. The proposal for the Sovereign Fund for Reindustrialization and Innovation, the FSRI (from its Portuguese name), is to turn that money into Brazilian factories, patents and platforms.

Chapter 1 · The Brazil that buys ready-made

We export raw materials and import what's worth more

Think of the chip in your phone, the equipment in your city's hospital, the battery that stores energy, the app that takes you home. Almost all of it is made, or controlled, abroad.

When Brazil depends on other countries for what is strategic, the value stays abroad too. The skilled jobs, the patents and the profit.

The proposal targets five fronts: information technology, semiconductors, medical equipment, advanced mining and mobility.

Chapter 2 · The secret of those who have a lot

Buy, borrow, never sell

People with very large fortunes almost never sell their own shares. Selling triggers capital gains tax. So the strategy is different, known abroad as Buy, Borrow, Die.

1
BuyAccumulate shares and stakes in companies.
2
BorrowUse those shares as collateral to take credit from the bank.
3
Never sell"Die": hold until the endLive on that credit. The wealth sits still and pays almost no tax.

Until 2025, this worked almost tax-free. Since 2026, anyone earning more than R$ 600,000 a year pays a minimum tax of up to 10% on income (Law 15,270 of 2025). But the same law leaves out real-estate and agribusiness credit notes (LCI and LCA), tax-exempt infrastructure bonds and real-estate funds. A very wealthy person can keep money in those and pay no minimum tax on them.

The most common reaction is: then let's tax large fortunes. The problem is that this idea usually hits two walls.

Why just taxing hits a wall
  • Capital moves. People change their tax residence and take their wealth with them.
  • Wealth isn't cash in the bank. Shares and stakes don't easily turn into tax without being sold.
What the FSRI does differently
  • It doesn't punish idle wealth. It gives it a reason to work here.
  • It uses the same loan that already exists, only pointed at new industry.

Chapter 3 · The turn

The path of the money

The investor keeps doing what they already do: borrowing with shares as collateral. What changes is where that money goes.

  1. 1
    The credit leaves the bankThe investor takes a loan with their own shares or stakes as collateral.
  2. 2
    It goes to approved projectsThe money goes into companies approved by a public FSRI panel, following the rules below.
  3. 3
    Part of the tax becomes investmentFor 5 years, the investment takes part off the investor's minimum tax. After 5 years, the FSRI profit comes out tax-free.
  4. 4
    The company grows and the investor exitsThe company lists on B3, the Brazilian stock exchange, or is sold. The investor sells their stake, pays off the loan and keeps what's left.

The rules, at a glance

For the investment to get the incentive
  • At least one Tier 1 project, with a quick return, and one Tier 3 project, in cutting-edge technology.
  • At least 40% of the investment in Tier 1. Housing doesn't count toward that 40%.
  • The money stays invested for at least 5 years.
  • No companies that compete with yours or sell to them. A system removes those from the list.
What the investor gets
  • A discount on the minimum tax. For 5 years, it takes off 2% of the amount invested each year, up to half of that year's minimum tax.
  • Tax-free profit. After 5 years, the FSRI profit is exempt and left out of the minimum tax, as already happens with LCA notes.
  • Insurance. If the company goes bankrupt, with an audit, the State returns part of the loss: up to 20%, 35% or 50%, depending on the tier.

Anyone who leaves before 5 years pays back the discount they used, and the profit pays normal tax. The shares used as collateral keep their usual tax.

Tier 1 makes a profit early and helps pay the loan interest. Tier 3 takes years, but that's where the chip and the technology the country lacks are. Without the rule, hardly anyone would choose the hard bet.

Chapter 4 · Risk of the right size

Three tiers, from the app to the chip

No serious investor puts money into a Brazilian chip that takes 15 years to pay off. Unless the risk is shared. That's why the State, through BNDES and FINEP (Brazil's development bank and innovation agency) and with the fund's own resources, comes in as a partial guarantor, and the protection grows with the risk.

Outside the tiers · its own track

Housing: low risk, long horizon

A housing building fits none of the tiers. The risk is lower than in Tier 1: the building is the collateral, some flats pay market price and the government pays the social part. But the building takes years to finish and decades to pay for itself. It works like an income real estate fund, with lower risk and capped profit.

If the project fails, who pays?

That's the question everyone should ask. The proposal's answer: the investor always pays most of it. Pick a tier and see how a loss splits, for every R$ 100 invested, in a project that truly went bankrupt.

In Tier 3, the insurance covers up to R$ 50. The investor bears at least R$ 50.

The investor loses first

Even in the riskiest tier, the investor bears at least half of the loss. Whoever risks their own money chooses and monitors better.

Audited bankruptcy only

The insurance doesn't cover profit that never came. It only pays out on a liquidation proven by an independent audit, with no sign of bad faith or misappropriation.

If it works, the State wins too

In successful Tier 3 projects, the State keeps 10% to 15%, in shares with special rights or a cut of sales, and that money goes back into the insurance fund.

No investing in yourself

Investing incentivized money in companies or sectors where the investor already has a relevant stake or control is forbidden. The fund creates competitors, not monopolies.

Chapter 5 · The investor's math

How much is left for the investor?

Nobody knows yet how much the FSRI would earn. It doesn't exist, so any number would be a guess.

What we can do is compare it with where big money already sits today and show what happens if everything goes wrong.

Who gets in

Instead of only taxing large fortunes, the FSRI gives them a reason to invest here. But the fund doesn't depend on them. If large fortunes don't want in, the pooled money of many people plays the same role.

Large wealth

People who already borrow from the bank with shares as collateral and pay the minimum tax. They change where a loan they'd take anyway goes. If the project fails, the debt with the bank remains.

Smaller wealth

Buys low-priced fund shares, capped at R$ 20,000 a year, as the CVM already does for startups (CVM Resolution 88 of 2022). Before getting in, they see a clear risk warning.

Who picks the companies

The investor builds their own portfolio from the projects approved by the public panel. Those who don't want to choose buy shares of a ready-made portfolio, built by a manager and supervised by the CVM, Brazil's securities regulator.

Either way, the investor doesn't run the company. The fund publishes open reports: where the money is, which milestone each company met and what each stake is worth.

How the money comes back

Shared profit

Tier 1 companies make a profit earlier and share part of it with partners. It helps pay the loan interest. Before 5 years, that profit pays normal tax.

Sale on the stock exchange

The company lists on B3 and the investor sells their stake.

Sale to another company

A bigger company buys the whole startup, and each partner gets their share.

Sale of the fund share

The investor sells their part of the fund to another investor.

Who this makes sense for

For people who pay little tax, fixed income is the right choice. It earns close to the CDI and has no risk of losing everything. The FSRI's smaller door is for those who want to back a Brazilian company and accept that risk.

For people with large wealth, the competition is LCA notes and tax-exempt infrastructure bonds. They are already exempt, left out of the minimum tax and almost risk-free. If the FSRI only matched the exemption, nobody would switch.

That's why the FSRI gives something extra: the discount on the minimum tax. It's money that would go to the government and becomes a factory, patent or platform here. The discount only exists if the investment follows the rules and stays 5 years.

Through the large door, the money comes from a loan, and the bank charges interest that tracks the CDI, plus a margin. In the average scenario, the FSRI earns a little more than the CDI. If the bank charges only the CDI, a little is left. If it charges a margin, what's left shrinks or disappears, and the tax discount helps close the gap.

What if everything goes wrong?

If every project in an investment fails, the insurance returns part of it and the investor loses the rest. In Tier 3, they lose at least half. In Tier 1, at least 80%.

In the CDI, the same money would have earned without that risk. That's why the FSRI isn't for anyone who needs the money back on a set date.

Chapter 6 · How an idea gets funded

From a real problem to the stock exchange

The money doesn't go to whoever knows somebody. It goes through a public funnel, in four stages. To see how it would work, follow an imaginary team.

Illustrative story. The team is made up; the stages and rules are those of the proposal.

  1. Stage 1 · National hackathon

    The challenge is published

    The State and the sector councils publish a concrete problem: develop Brazilian hospital sensors.

    A team of three, an engineer, a nursing technician and a programmer, signs up. For the first time, a team like that has an open path to investors.

  2. Stage 2 · Acceleration · 4 months

    From paper to prototype

    The team wins and receives seed capital, in the range of R$ 200,000 to R$ 500,000, plus mentoring at an institution of excellence, such as ITA, Unicamp, USP, Fiocruz, CEITEC or a federal university.

    In four months, out comes a working prototype and an audited business plan.

  3. Stage 3 · Public panel

    A technical panel, live

    The team pitches the project in a session streamed for everyone to see. On the panel: senior engineers, sector experts and the investors qualified in the fund.

    Anyone can watch and understand why one project was approved and another wasn't.

  4. Stage 4 · Release by milestones

    The money arrives in parts

    The approved investment doesn't land in the account all at once. Each part is only released when the team meets a technical, regulatory and financial milestone, checked by an audit.

  5. Years later

    A Brazilian sensor in your city's hospital

    The company matures and goes public on B3. The investor sells their stake, pays off the loan and profits. The patent is registered in Brazil. The jobs are here.

Chapter 7 · Fair competition

When the giant sells below cost

A new Brazilian platform usually fails not because it's worse, but because it faces a well-known game: winner takes all.

First

A foreign giant charges below cost for years and absorbs the loss.

Then

Local competitors can't keep losing money that long and close down.

In the end

With no competition, monopoly and high fees follow, for those who work and those who consume.

↓

The antidote: an automatic defense

CADE sees it

Brazil's antitrust authority cross-checks electronic invoices and Pix data to monitor margins and average prices in each sector, in each city.

The fee triggers on its own

If a company systematically operates below cost to eliminate competitors, the CIDE-Concorrencial, a competition levy, applies to its transactions in that city.

The money levels the field

What is collected reduces taxes and the fees of the national platform under attack, for as long as the attack lasts.

The more the giant sells below cost, the more money goes to its Brazilian competitor.

Who wins

Tier 1 platforms are born connected to Gov.br, the federal government's digital ID, and to Pix. That slashes the cost of winning each customer and lets them charge lower fees than the big foreign platforms.

An example that already exists: galera.club, a Brazilian link-in-bio page, with Pix on the page and no fee per sale.

The driver

Keeps a bigger share of each ride.

The event producer

Sells tickets without handing a huge cut to the ticketing platform.

The consumer

Pays lower fees and gets real competition.

The researcher

Sees the lab become a product, with a patent registered in Brazil.

The investor

Swaps part of the tax for productive investment and exits through the Brazilian stock exchange.

The country

Depends less on others and keeps the value that today goes away.

Chapter 8 · The criticism

The toughest criticism, and the answers

These are the objections I'd expect to hear, answered with the proposal's own rules.

Why would I risk my wealth on companies I don't know and don't control?

If you need that money, you shouldn't. Brazilian funds of this kind earned, on average, only a little more than the CDI, and the gap between the best and the worst was huge (Insper, Spectra and ABVCAP, 2022).

The FSRI doesn't promise a return. It changes the math: the insurance cuts part of the loss, the discount and the exemption raise what's left, and a portfolio of several projects spreads the risk. People with smaller wealth get in with a yearly cap. The comparison with other investments is in chapter 5.

This is a tax break for the rich.

Today the rich already get exemptions without giving anything back: LCI, LCA, tax-exempt infrastructure bonds and real-estate funds are left out of the minimum tax (Law 15,270 of 2025).

The FSRI asks for more. The discount only applies if the money becomes real investment in the country, stays 5 years and includes a cutting-edge technology project. And it has a cap: at most half of that year's minimum tax.

No new factory, patent or platform, no benefit.

The State will foot the bill when it goes wrong.

The insurance has a cap: 20%, 35% or 50%, depending on the risk, and 10% for housing. The investor bears at least half of the loss, even in the worst case.

The insurance is only triggered by an audited bankruptcy, with no bad faith. And when a high-risk project succeeds, the State keeps 10% to 15% to refill the fund.

It will become a handout for the same old groups.

Selection is public: open hackathon, a panel streamed live and money released only against audited milestones.

And investors can't put incentivized money into a company or sector they already run. The fund exists to create competitors.

An automatic levy on foreign companies scares off investment.

The CIDE-Concorrencial doesn't apply to a foreign company for being foreign. It only applies once systematic below-cost operation aimed at eliminating competition is found, and only in that location.

Whoever competes honestly pays nothing.

Brazilian chips? Brazil can't compete in that.

That's why Tier 3 has the longest horizon, 10 to 15 years, and the most protection. And that's why it's mandatory: without it, nobody would choose the hard bet.

Pairing a quick project with a slow one ensures the investor gets a quick return in Tier 1 while waiting for Tier 3 to mature.

The tax discount is money the government stops collecting.

True, and that's why it has a cap. The discount is at most 2% of the investment per year, for 5 years, and never more than half of that year's minimum tax.

In return, the money stays in Brazil, in a new company, for at least 5 years. Anyone who leaves early pays the discount back.

Chapter 9 · How it leaves the paper

The roadmap to becoming law

Federal · Chamber of Deputies

A Complementary Law bill that creates the FSRI and defines:

  • the discount on the minimum tax and the exemption of profit after 5 years, only for investments that follow the rules;
  • the insurance through BNDES and Finep, with a cap per tier;
  • the CIDE-Concorrencial trigger within the Brazilian Competition Defense System (Law 12.529/2011).
State · Legislative Assembly of Goiás
  • A State Fund for Co-investment and Industrial Innovation, as a regional arm;
  • rules for the State to buy innovative solutions;
  • a Goiás Circuit of Industrial Hackathons, with FAPEG, UFG and the productive sector.

Starting with Goiás

The model can get a regional arm, building on what Goiás is already good at.

North and Northeast Goiás

Rare earths with added value

Goiás has significant reserves of critical minerals and rare earths, such as in Minaçu and the surrounding region. Tier 2 and 3 investments can fund local refining, instead of exporting only raw ore.

Anápolis · DAIA

Medicine and equipment made here

Use the pharmaceutical cluster of the Anápolis Agro-Industrial District to make active pharmaceutical ingredients and medical and hospital equipment.

Southwest Goiás

From agribusiness to agtech

Hackathons on precision agriculture, drones, soil sensors and bio-inputs, with UFG, IF Goiano and research centers. Agribusiness capital funds new technology, with no conflict of interest.

The whole state

Brazilian technology on trial

Pilot programs, through public procurement of innovation under Brazil's Startup Legal Framework, to test digital notary offices, mobility and public management created in local innovation marathons.
