Proposal under study

The money to reindustrialize Brazil already exists. It's just sitting still.

People with large fortunes already borrow from banks using their own shares as collateral. The proposal for the Sovereign Fund for Reindustrialization and Innovation, the FSRI (from its Portuguese name), is to turn that money into Brazilian factories, patents and platforms.

Chapter 1 · The Brazil that buys ready-made

We export raw materials and import what's worth more

Think of the chip in your phone, the equipment in your city's hospital, the battery that stores energy, the app that takes you home. Almost all of it is made, or controlled, abroad.

When Brazil depends on other countries for what is strategic, the value stays abroad too. The skilled jobs, the patents and the profit.

The proposal targets five fronts: information technology, semiconductors, medical equipment, advanced mining and mobility.

Chapter 2 · The secret of those who have a lot

Buy, borrow, never sell

People with very large fortunes almost never sell their own shares. Selling triggers capital gains tax. So the strategy is different, known abroad as Buy, Borrow, Die: buy, borrow, and leave it as an inheritance.

1
BuyAccumulate shares and stakes in companies.
2
BorrowUse those shares as collateral to take credit from the bank.
3
Never sell"Die": hold until the endLive on that credit. The wealth sits still and pays almost no tax.

Since 2026, anyone earning more than R$ 600,000 a year pays a minimum income tax that reaches 10% from R$ 1.2 million (Law 15,270 of 2025). But some things are left out of it: LCI and LCA notes (bank bonds tied to real estate and agribusiness), tax-exempt infrastructure bonds and real-estate funds.

The most common reaction is: then let's tax large fortunes. The problem is that this idea usually hits two walls.

Why just taxing hits a wall
  • Capital moves. People change their tax residence and take their wealth with them.
  • Wealth isn't cash in the bank. Shares and stakes don't easily turn into tax without being sold.
What the FSRI does differently
  • It doesn't punish idle wealth. It gives it a reason to work here.
  • It uses the same loan that already exists, only pointed at new industry.

Chapter 3 · The turn

The path of the money

The investor keeps borrowing with shares as collateral. Only the money's destination changes.

  1. 1
    The credit leaves the bankWith the investor's shares as collateral.
  2. 2
    It goes to approved companiesProjects picked by a public FSRI panel.
  3. 3
    Part of the tax becomes investmentFor 5 years, the investment takes part off the minimum tax. After that, the profit comes out tax-free.
  4. 4
    The investor exitsThe company lists on B3, the Brazilian stock exchange, or is sold. They sell their stake and pay off the loan.

The risk tiers and the incentive rules come in the next chapter.

Chapter 4 · Risk of the right size

Three tiers, from the app to the chip

Nobody puts money into a Brazilian chip that takes 15 years to pay off, unless the risk is shared. That's why the State, through BNDES and Finep (Brazil's development bank and innovation agency), covers part of the loss. The higher the risk, the more protection.

The rules, at a glance

For the investment to get the incentive
  • One Tier 1 project, with a quick return, and one Tier 3 project, in cutting-edge technology.
  • At least 40% of the investment in Tier 1. Housing doesn't count.
  • At least 5 years invested.
  • No competitors or suppliers of your own companies.
What the investor gets
  • A minimum-tax discount: 2% of the investment a year, for 5 years, up to half of that year's tax.
  • Tax-free profit after 5 years, left out of the minimum tax, like LCA notes.
  • Insurance for 20%, 35% or 50% of the loss, depending on the tier, in an audited bankruptcy.

Anyone who leaves before 5 years pays back the discount. Shares used as collateral keep their usual tax.

If the project fails, who pays?

The investor always pays most of it. Pick a tier and see how a loss splits, for every R$ 100, in a project that truly went bankrupt.

If it works, the State wins too

In Tier 3 projects that succeed, the State keeps 10% to 15%, which goes back into the insurance fund.

Chapter 5 · The investor's math

How much is left for the investor?

The FSRI doesn't exist, so any return figure would be a guess. What we can do is compare it with where big money already sits today.

Who gets in

Large wealth

Already borrows with shares as collateral and pays the minimum tax. If the project fails, the debt with the bank remains.

Smaller wealth

Buys fund shares capped at R$ 20,000 a year, as the CVM already does for startups (CVM Resolution 88 of 2022).

The investor picks companies from the approved list, or buys a ready-made portfolio built by a manager supervised by the CVM, Brazil's securities regulator. They get paid when the company shares profit, lists on B3 or is sold.

Who this makes sense for

For people who pay little tax, fixed income is the right choice: it earns close to the CDI with no risk of losing everything.

For large wealth, the competition is LCA notes and infrastructure bonds, which are already tax-exempt and nearly risk-free. That's why the FSRI gives the minimum-tax discount. Through the large door, loan interest eats almost all the gain. In the average scenario, the discount may close the gap. In the bad one, you lose. The simulator does the math with your bank's interest.

What if everything goes wrong?

The insurance returns part of it and the investor loses the rest: at least half in Tier 3 and 80% in Tier 1, before the tax discount. That's why the FSRI isn't for anyone who needs the money on a set date.

Chapter 6 · How an idea gets funded

From a real problem to the stock exchange

The money doesn't go to whoever knows someone. It goes through a public funnel, in four stages. Follow an imaginary team.

An illustrative story. The team is made up; the stages and rules are the proposal's.

  1. Stage 1 · National innovation marathon

    The challenge is published

    The government publishes a concrete problem: Brazilian hospital sensors. An engineer, a nursing technician and a programmer sign up.

  2. Stage 2 · Acceleration · 4 months

    From paper to prototype

    The team wins and gets R$ 200,000 to R$ 500,000, plus mentoring at a university or research institute. In four months, they have a prototype and an audited business plan.

  3. Stage 3 · Public panel

    A technical panel, live

    Engineers, specialists and investors assess the project in a streamed session. Everyone sees why one project passed and another didn't.

  4. Stage 4 · Release by milestones

    The money arrives in parts

    Each part is only released when the team meets a milestone checked by an audit.

  5. Years later

    A Brazilian sensor in your city's hospital

    The company lists on B3. The investor sells their stake and pays off the loan. The patent and the jobs stay here.

Chapter 7 · Fair competition

When the giant sells below cost

A new Brazilian platform usually fails because of a familiar game. A giant charges below cost for years, competitors close, and then come the monopoly and the high fees.

The antidote: an automatic defense

CADE sees it

Brazil's competition authority cross-checks electronic invoices and Pix to track prices and margins in each sector, in each city.

The levy kicks in

Whoever sells below cost to break competitors pays the CIDE-Concorrencial, a new contribution, in that city.

The game evens out

The money collected lowers the fees of the Brazilian platform under attack.

Who wins

Tier 1 platforms are born connected to Gov.br, the federal government's digital ID, and to Pix. That lowers the cost of each customer and lets them charge lower fees. One example that already exists is galera.club.

Workers

Drivers and event producers keep more of each ride and each ticket.

The consumer

Pays lower fees and gets competition.

The researcher

Sees the lab become a product, with a patent in Brazil.

The country

Depends less on others.

Chapter 8 · The criticism

The toughest criticism, and the answers

Why would I risk my wealth on companies I don't know and don't control?

If you need that money, you shouldn't. Funds like this earned only a little more than the CDI, with a big gap between good and bad ones.

The FSRI doesn't promise a return. The insurance cuts part of the loss, and the discount and exemption raise what's left. Compare in chapter 5.

This is a tax break for the rich.

Today the rich already have exempt bonds without giving anything back: LCI, LCA, infrastructure bonds and real-estate funds are left out of the minimum tax (Law 15,270 of 2025).

The FSRI only gives a discount if the money becomes a factory, patent or platform here, for 5 years.

The tax discount is money the government stops collecting.

That's why it has a cap: at most 2% of the investment a year and half of that year's minimum tax.

Anyone who leaves before 5 years pays it back.

The State will foot the bill when it goes wrong.

The insurance has a cap, and the investor bears at least half of the loss.

When a Tier 3 project succeeds, the State keeps 10% to 15%.

It will become a handout for the same old groups.

Selection is open, the panel is streamed live and money comes out by audited milestones.

And nobody invests in competitors or suppliers of their own companies.

An automatic levy on foreign companies scares off investment.

The CIDE-Concorrencial doesn't look at where a company is from.

It only applies to whoever sells below cost to break competitors, and only in that city.

Brazilian chips? Brazil can't compete in that.

That's why Tier 3 has the longest horizon and the most protection, and is mandatory.

Without the rule, nobody would choose the hard bet.

Chapter 10 · How it leaves the paper

The roadmap to becoming law

Federal · Chamber of Deputies

A bill that creates the FSRI and changes the minimum-tax law (article 16-A of Law 9,250), with an estimate of the cost to the government and a yearly cap. It defines:

  • the discount on the minimum tax and the exemption of profit after 5 years, only for investments that follow the rules;
  • the insurance through BNDES and Finep, with a cap per tier;
  • the CIDE-Concorrencial trigger within the Brazilian Competition Defense System (Law 12.529/2011).
State · Legislative Assembly of Goiás
  • A State Fund for Co-investment and Industrial Innovation, as a regional arm;
  • rules for the State to buy innovative solutions;
  • a Goiás Circuit of Industrial Hackathons, with FAPEG, UFG and the productive sector.
