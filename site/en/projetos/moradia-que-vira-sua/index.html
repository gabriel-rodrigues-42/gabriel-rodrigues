Idea

The rent that becomes your home.

People who live in a risk area or have nowhere to live get a safe flat near the bus, with a garden and a grocery store on the ground floor. The first year is free. After that, part of the rent buys the flat.

This page brings together what Brazil has already tried, what worked in other countries and a first version of a proposal, with Goiânia as the first example. The money comes from two places: the reindustrialization fund (FSRI) builds, and a public housing fund pays the social part.

Chapter 1 · How it works today

Families leave the risk and end up far from everything

  1. 1
    The house sits where the water flowsWith nowhere to live near work, families settle on stream banks or slopes. Nobody measures the risk before the house goes up.
    Bottleneck: the risk only shows up in the flood
  2. 2
    Removal comes with rent aidThe city moves the family out and pays temporary rent while they wait for a permanent home.
  3. 3
    The housing estate goes where land is cheapAn Ipea study of the 20 largest cities, Goiânia among them, found that Minha Casa, Minha Vida estates ended up further from jobs than the favelas the families came from.
    Bottleneck: new home, two hours on the bus
  4. 4
    The family is tied to the addressThe program's law forbids selling or renting out the flat. People who change jobs or cities have no legal way out. In the old lease program (PAR), this turned into informal "drawer contracts" and court cases.
    Bottleneck: whoever needs to move loses everything or goes informal

Chapter 3 · The proposal

One free year, then rent that turns into a purchase

  1. 1
    First year without payingFor people leaving a risk area or an occupation without papers. It is time to settle into the new place.
  2. 2
    Then a payment that fits the incomeFrom 10% of income, for those who earn least, up to 30%, for those who earn most. Never more than 30%, the same limit used in the United States and Singapore.
  3. 3
    Half of the payment goes to the purchaseThat money is saved in the family's name. The public fund covers the rest of the price, as Minha Casa, Minha Vida already does.
  4. 4
    Need to move? Take the money with youThe family takes what it saved to another flat in the program. Before 5 years, it takes 90%. After 5 years, it takes all of it. The discount is there to discourage people who join only to leave.
  5. 5
    Stopped paying? The savings cover it firstThe saved money pays the instalments until it runs out. Nobody is evicted by surprise. Only when the balance reaches zero does eviction follow today's rules.

Chapter 4 · Who goes first

A queue that starts with lives in danger

  1. 1
    Immediate risk to lifeA slope giving way, a house that floods to the ceiling. That is an emergency and jumps any queue.
  2. 2
    People living on the streetThey come in through a "Housing First" track: the home first, then health and job support. At most 1 in 10 flats in each building, so there is support without concentration.
  3. 3
    People with nowhere to liveFamilies staying with relatives, sharing one room or spending much of their income on rent.
  4. 4
    Other families in risk areasIn the order of the risk map, from most to least dangerous.

Only people already registered before the project is announced can join. Anyone who occupies an area after that does not enter the queue. It is the same logic as the cut-off date in land regularization.

Chapter 5 · Where to build

A yardstick so nobody is pushed far away

"Easy access to transport" has to become a number, or cheap, distant land wins again. The yardstick below comes from the international standard for transit-oriented neighbourhoods (ITDP). Land that fails it gets no money from the program.

  • 1 kmon foot
    To a BRT or rapid-transit station. In Goiânia, the Eixo Anhanguera and the 29.6 km North-South BRT are the backbone.
  • 500 mon foot
    To a stop with a bus at least every 15 minutes, from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m.
  • 500 mon foot
    To a place that sells fresh food, and to a park.
  • 1 kmon foot
    To a daycare or school, and to a clinic or pharmacy.
  • Outsidethe risk
    Never in a risk area on the Goiânia map or the Geological Survey of Brazil's.

It can be a little further from downtown, as long as it passes the yardstick. The ITDP standard also suggests rehousing families within 500 m of where they lived, when there is safe land nearby. That way the child does not change schools and the adult does not change jobs.

Chapter 6 · What the buildings could look like

Three models, with gardens and shops on the ground floor

The drawings are examples to talk about, not architectural projects. Tap a part of the building to see what it does.

For neighbourhoods near a station

Two low buildings around an open courtyard. It is the cheapest design to build and maintain, and it fits mid-sized plots along the North-South BRT.

For corridors like the Eixo Anhanguera

A long building right on the sidewalk, with the whole ground floor in shops. It puts more families where transport already exists.

For empty downtown buildings

Retrofitting an idle building is faster than building new, and families live where jobs, schools and buses already are. São Paulo's first downtown housing PPP, in 2015, followed this idea.

The ground-floor shops

  • The shops belong to the fund and are never sold. In Singapore, the government sold more than half of the shops in its housing estates and is now considering buying some back, because rents there rose more.
  • Grocery and pharmacy come first. Every building reserves space for food and medicine. Daycares and clinics come in when the neighbourhood needs them.
  • Tenants are chosen on price and quality. Singapore does this with supermarkets: both the bid and the proposal count, such as the company's track record and what it offers the neighbourhood.
  • Lower rent at the start. Up to 6 months rent-free and a discount in the first years, as Singapore has done since March 2026. The shop opens as soon as families arrive.

Mixed incomes in the same building

The building is not only for low-income families. In Chile, a project in the social integration program reserves at least 25% of the flats for very poor families and up to 60% for other incomes. Those who earn more pay more and help pay for the building. Nobody can tell from the door who pays what.

Chapter 7 · Where the money comes from

Investors build, the public fund pays the social part

Investors want their money back. The free year and the cheap shop rents return nothing. So the bill has two owners.

FSRI

Builds and gets paid back

  • The investment comes in as a Tier 1 project, the lowest-risk one.
  • It is repaid by the families' instalments and the shop rents, over a long period.
  • If there is a loss, the investor loses first, under the FSRI rules.
Public fund

Pays for the social support

  • The free first year.
  • The gap between the family's payment and the cost of the flat.
  • Social work with families and "Housing First" support.
  • It can come from the funds that already pay for Minha Casa, Minha Vida (FAR and FNHIS).

Brazil has done something similar. In São Paulo's first downtown housing PPP, signed in 2015, the company invested about R$ 900 million in 3,683 homes. The state government pays about R$ 465 million (in 2015 values) over 20 years, and only after each delivery.

Chapter 8 · The place left empty

Where there was risk, a park grows

The area the family left becomes a park or forest in the same month as the move. It joins the sponge city plan: the ground holds rainwater again, and flooding drops for the whole neighbourhood.

Brazil's land regularization law already requires the government to relocate families when the risk cannot be fixed with works. What is missing is a good place to go. When the risk can be fixed, homes on safe ground are regularized right there, with free registration.

Chapter 9 · Abroad

What worked in other countries

Singapore

Almost everyone lives in public housing

76% of residents live in buildings of the public housing agency, and 73% in a flat they bought. The mortgage payment cannot exceed 30% of income.

HDB, 2024/2025 statistics

England

Buying in shares

The family buys 10% to 75% of the home and pays rent on the rest, at most 3% a year of the share it does not own. Then it buys more, 1% at a time, and the rent falls.

UK Government, 2026

Vienna

Social housing for the middle class too

44% of the city's homes are municipal or belong to limited-profit associations. It is not only for the poor.

City of Vienna, 2017 census data

Chile

Mixed incomes and rent with a promise to buy

The integration program mixes incomes in the same project, in neighbourhoods with services. Another program rents the home with a promise of sale.

Chile's Housing Ministry

Finland

Housing first

People living on the street get a flat with their own lease, and support comes after. The number of homeless people fell from over 18,000 in 1987 to 7,898 in 2015.

World Habitat, with data from Finland's housing agency (ARA), 2016

United States

Rent at 30% of income

In public housing, the family pays 30% of adjusted income. The rule has been in place since 1981.

US Congressional Research Service (CRS), 2017

Chapter 10 · Criticism

The hard questions

Doesn't a free year encourage land invasions?

Only people already registered before the project is announced can join. Anyone who occupies land afterwards does not. And the cleared area becomes a park right after the move, so there is no spot left to occupy again.

Why not just give the flat away?

Minha Casa, Minha Vida almost gives the home away, but forbids selling it, and the family is tied to the address. Here the payment becomes savings in the family's name. If they need to change jobs or cities, they take the money with them, without informal contracts.

Isn't this just Minha Casa, Minha Vida under another name?

It uses the same funds and the same priority for risk areas, which are already in the law. It changes four things: the location yardstick becomes mandatory, the ground floor has shops, families can move without losing what they paid, and the building mixes incomes.

Won't mixed-income buildings lead to conflict?

The building rules are the same for everyone, and management is professional. Chile has done this for years. I still need to study the results there before saying how well it worked.

What if families stop paying and the investor loses?

Each family's savings cover the instalments first. If that is not enough, the FSRI rule applies: the investor loses first, and in Tier 1 bears at least 80% of the loss. The public fund does not cover the investor's loss.

Won't it push people far away again?

That is what the yardstick in chapter 5 is for. Land without a station within 1 km, a grocery within 500 m and a school within 1 km gets no money. The rule goes into the law and does not depend on the goodwill of each government.

Chapter 11 · How to measure

Numbers to check every year

  • How many flats are within 1 km of a station.
  • How long families take to get to work, before and after the move.
  • How many families finished buying their flat.
  • How many families fell behind on payments and how many were evicted.
  • How many risk areas became parks, and whether anyone moved back in.
Chapter 12 · The road

From paper to the first building

  1. 1
    Change the Minha Casa, Minha Vida lawCreate rent that turns into a purchase, with savings the family takes if it moves, and put the location yardstick into law. The law already allows social rent and leasing (Law 14,620/2023, art. 4).
  2. 2
    Bring housing into the FSRIHousing with ground-floor shops comes in as a Tier 1 project.
  3. 3
    A pilot in GoiâniaOne building near the North-South BRT or the Eixo Anhanguera, for families from the risk areas on the sponge city map.
